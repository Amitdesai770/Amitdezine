THE PROBLEM

The Checkout That Couldn't Keep Up

"Checkout — the most revenue-critical moment in the entire journey — was running on infrastructure we didn't control."

  • Sale events pushed checkout past its limits — every session firing off a stream of API calls
  • Shopify capped how many could get through, then started throttling the rest
  • The result landed exactly where it hurt most: slower responses, occasional instability, right at the moment of purchase

The real issue sat one layer deeper.

Infrastructure We Didn't Own

We were running our most performance-sensitive, revenue-critical moment inside someone else's system — which is exactly why checkout became the first thing we chose to take back.

OBSERVATIONS

Shopify's Four Breaking Points

Four separate pressures were converging inside Shopify's checkout, all at once:

System

Rate Limits

Peak load throttled the API; checkout slowed and destabilized.

Experience

Limited UI Control

No brand alignment; key flows were hard to iterate on.

Observability

No Funnel Data

Drop-offs were invisible; nothing to iterate against.

Business Risk

No Infrastructure Control

The most revenue-critical touchpoint, running on someone else's rules.

Assessment Context

User Problem

Speed was the whole promise of checkout — and under real load, that promise broke. Users hit a flow that slowed down and grew less predictable exactly when they were ready to pay, with hesitation creeping in at the worst possible moment.

Business Problem

For the team, this wasn't just about optimizing a screen — it was about earning back control. Speed, visibility, and a checkout built to hold up as the business scales, not just survive it.

DESIGN DIRECTION

Three Goals, No Room for Drift

01
Minimize drop-off at the single most critical step in the purchase journey
02
Reduce friction and cognitive load during payment selection
03
Shorten the path from selection to confirmation as much as possible

The Three Things Users Actually Wanted to Know

  • What's the total?
  • When will it arrive?

    • Every address renders as its own compact, scannable card — read in a glance, not studied.

    Selecting an address updates the estimated delivery date immediately — the consequence of that choice is never a mystery.

    Until an address is confirmed, the payment section stays visibly muted — the same rule that governs the rest of the flow: nothing moves forward on an unconfirmed step.

    Delivery dates stay hidden until the next screen, or the orders page after purchase — too late to matter at the moment someone's deciding.

    The address sits in plain text, no different from anything else on the page — easy to miss, easier to ignore.

    When There's No Address on File

    • Most addresses arrive automatically — pulled from Shiprocket right after login
    • Occasionally, none exist yet
    • When that happens, we ask for one before moving forward
    • The payment CTA stays locked until a valid address is in place

    Letting the Map Do the Typing

    • Manual address entry was replaced with a map-first flow
    • Search a location, drop a pin, the form fills itself in
    • Less typing for the user, more precision for delivery

    Making Sure the Pin Means It

    A map makes address entry faster — but it also opens two ways for a location to be wrong without anyone noticing. Both needed a safety net before checkout could trust them.

    Safeguard 1 — Location Mismatch

    Move the pin away from the address that was actually searched, and the flow catches it — nudging the user to re-align before they can continue.

    ⚠️ "This pin's a little off — drag it to match your searched address before continuing."

    Safeguard 2 — Location Unserviceable

    Land somewhere outside the delivery zone, and the flow says so directly, then locks the CTA until the address is fixed.

    🚫 "We don't deliver here yet — try a different address to continue."

2. Payment Selection

Here's the version that shipped, and what led to it.

Store credit and gift cards surface first — so the amount someone's actually paying is settled before they even choose how to pay it.

Every payment method lives in one flat list — no toggle to set first, no category to expand into. Where V3 fragmented the flow and V2 cluttered it, this removes the extra decision entirely.

Selecting a method and paying happen in one continuous glance down the screen — nothing to jump back and forth between.

The idea: ask payment intent before showing payment options — Pay Now or Pay on Delivery, decided upfront.

It clarified the decision technically — but it added a step instead of removing one. Set the toggle, then jump to a second area to actually pick a method. Clarity at the cost of friction — the toggle and the payment options never felt like the same interaction.

The goal was to cut decision time by surfacing the most common option first — a single recommended method, pushed to the top.

Then the data disagreed: 41% of users pay with COD, 21% with PhonePe — the option being recommended wasn't the option people actually chose. A recommendation nobody asked for, sitting above three more layers — categories, then providers — added weight instead of removing it.

The first move was the safest one: tighten the layout, group what already existed, keep the pattern familiar.

It read cleaner. But the underlying hierarchy never got questioned — same defaults, same order, just less visual noise around them. A polish pass, not a redesign.

What Two Weeks Actually Bought Us

Here's what changed once it shipped:

+2.68% increase

— in checkout conversion.

25.7% faster

— checkout completion; average time to convert dropped to roughly 2 minutes.

94% address completion success

— among users who followed the normal address flow, 94% successfully saved their address and continued checkout.

55.7% recovery after drop-offs

— even when users backed out of the map step, 55.7% returned and completed the address form, meaning the new interface caused only temporary hesitation, not a blocker.

Two weeks, a platform migration, and a checkout that finally answers to us instead of Shopify.
