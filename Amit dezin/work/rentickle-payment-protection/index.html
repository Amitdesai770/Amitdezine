Rental Commerce

Simplifying Payment and Protection Decisions in Rentickle

Moving advance payment and damage protection upfront to clarify rental commitments before checkout.

Rentickle is India's leading furniture and appliance rental platform. Instead of heavy upfront purchases, it lets users rent high-quality lifestyle goods through affordable monthly payments, offering flexibility for modern, mobile urban consumers.

Role Product Designer
Timeline 1 Month
Tools Figma, GPT, Miro
Team Freelance project — Me (Product Designer), 1 Senior Designer, 1 Developer

Rental tenure and damage protection configuration interface: surfacing advance payment savings and deposit protections upfront.

Business

Rentickle is India's leading furniture and appliance rental platform. Instead of heavy upfront purchases, it lets users rent high-quality lifestyle goods through affordable monthly payments, offering flexibility for modern, mobile urban consumers.

Challenge

Checkout failed due to late-stage surprises. Advance payment and damage protection stayed hidden until the final step, tied to backend credit checks. Vague terms meant users only learned about deposit deductions after rental ended.

This broke trust, drove cart abandonment, and forced a 24–48 hour manual verification delay.

Solution

Redesigned the configuration engine to move advance and protection decisions upfront. A modular, item-by-item system surfaces pricing and damage protection on the product page and in the cart, allowing users to set tenure and coverage before checkout.

Reported impact summary

Confirmed Orders ~2× Reported increase in confirmed orders
Protection Plan ~82% Reported adoption of Protection Plan
Advance Plan ~9% Reported adoption; lifted total order revenue
02

The Problem

Rentickle's checkout made important financial decisions difficult to understand before a user committed to an order. Advance-payment options depended on backend credit checks, while the terms and possible damage deductions were not explained clearly enough upfront.

Users could believe they had completed an order, only to discover that further verification or decisions were still required. Damage-related deductions and refund adjustments were also difficult to anticipate until the rental ended.

The resulting experience reduced transparency and user control while creating uncertainty, additional support needs, possible cart abandonment and manual delays of 24–48 hours.

Advance payment: users did not know what they could choose

Among high-risk users—specifically users with low CIBIL or credit scores and users facing document-related issues—participation in advance payment showed a notable gap:

Research identified three primary reasons behind this low participation:

  • Low awareness: Users were often unaware that advance payment existed and how it worked.
  • Poor understanding of the benefits: The financial savings and advantages of paying in advance were not clearly communicated.
  • Lack of confidence: Friction, complexity, or trust concerns during the flow caused users to hesitate or disengage.

Uncertainty after order placement

The original order flow introduced significant friction after order submission. The "Order Flow After Placement" data identified five distinct operational outcomes:

  • Approved: Immediate confirmation for qualifying orders.
  • Low CIBIL Score: Orders diverted for risk verification.
  • Documentation Issues: Incomplete or unverified identity records requiring follow-up.
  • Other Factors: Additional operational or address validation requirements.
  • Risk Flags: Internal security or tenure risk parameters.

The key insight is that a significant number of users did not receive instant approval. Many were sent into manual review without transparent tracking or status updates, creating anxiety and uncertainty after users thought their order was already complete.

Protection and damage settlement

Under the original model, customers only learned about potential damage charges at the end of their subscription. During pickup and physical inspection, potential damage costs were calculated and compared against the refundable security deposit to determine deductions and final settlements. Because the deduction rules and refund logic were not explained beforehand, customers experienced unexpected deposit deductions, creating friction at the very end of their customer lifecycle.

Existing Workflows

The original order journey depended on a decision that happened outside the checkout interface. Manual credit review and offline customer-service calls left users uncertain about their payment options, pricing and order status.

Original advance-payment and credit-review flow

The operational sequence was split across three distinct swimlanes:

  • User Swimlane: Browses products and adds items to the cart $ o$ Places an order and completes KYC $ o$ Encounters choices involving E-NACH only, full advance payment, or partial advance payment plus E-NACH $ o$ Proceeds through payment or mandate setup.
  • Credit Team Swimlane: Conducts manual CIBIL checks with high turnaround times. If CIBIL is at least 750, Customer Service contacts the user offline to explain available options (without prior pricing visibility at decision point). If CIBIL is below 750, the team performs a manual risk review to decide the payment method.
  • System / Payments Swimlane: Creates the order with a "Pending review" status (a known high drop-off point) $ o$ Redirects user to PayU or E-NACH $ o$ Processes gateway outcome. On success, order is approved; on failure, order enters manual review and may be approved or cancelled.

Original damage and settlement flow

The original damage-handling sequence followed eight consecutive stages:

1. Pickup Initiated → 2. Item Collected → 3. Condition Inspected → 4. No Damage Confirmation OR 5. Damage Cost Calculated → 6. Compared with Deposit → 7. Deducted / Adjusted → 8. Final Refund / Settlement

Competitive research takeaways

  • Entry points vary across the funnel: CityFurnish and Furlenco introduce advance payment in the cart, whereas Rentomojo surfaces it directly on the product detail page.
  • Protection availability vs policy clarity: Most competitors offer protection plans, but they vary significantly in how clearly refund rules, extra charges, and final commitments are disclosed.
  • Variations in user control: Furlenco provides the highest overall user control through item-level flexibility, whereas Rentomojo and Fabrento offer more restricted control.

Competitive positioning map

Platforms were mapped across two core dimensions: User-led control (low to high) and Flexibility versus rigidity (rigid to flexible).

  • CityFurnish: Upper-right position; high user control and flexibility.
  • Furlenco: Upper-left to centre; moderate-to-high user control with a more structured experience.
  • Rentomojo: Lower-right; flexible options but lower user-led control.
  • Fabrento and Rentickle: Lower-left; low user control and rigid experiences.

Insights

Across advance payment and protection, the recurring problem was not simply a missing feature. Users were being asked to make important financial decisions without enough information, at the point when changing their decision was most difficult.

Finding 01

Unclear benefits and coverage

Users lacked a clear upfront understanding of advance-payment benefits and what the protection plan covered.

Finding 02

Late cost disclosure

Pricing details, savings and damage-adjustment logic appeared too late in the rental experience.

Finding 03

Limited user control

Key decisions were driven by manual or system processes rather than being available as clear user choices.

Finding 04

Operational delays

Heavy dependency on manual intervention caused 24–48 hour delays in order processing.

Finding 05

Unclear end-of-rental expectations

Users were not prepared for pickup, damage assessment and potential refund adjustments.

Finding 06

Loss of trust

Late-stage surprises created confusion, anxiety and a weaker sense of control over commitments.

Three core design decisions

Decision 1 — Advance payment visibility at product level

Users can see advance-payment options and associated savings for each tenure on the PDP before checkout.

Decision 2 — Protection plan at cart

The cart surfaces the protection option and clearer coverage information. Users can understand the benefit and turn the plan off if they do not need it.

Decision 3 — Per-item customisation instead of cart-wide settings

Users can customise the payment and protection preferences of each product individually.

Redesigned end-to-end cart flow

The cart becomes the place where users review and adjust rental preferences without navigating through multiple pages. Each product retains its own tenure, protection and payment settings, while changes update the relevant item-level breakdown and grand total.

  • Cart item card: Displays product image, name, remove action, refundable deposit, rent and price, tenure selection, Care Plan selection, Payment Plan selection, and edit action.
  • Edit drawer / item preferences: Modal drawer supporting adjustments for tenure, Care Plan, Payment Plan (including advance payment options), and quantity.
  • State updates: Totals, rent, deposit, Care Plan, and the item-level cost breakdown update after saving changes.
07

Design

The solution materializes through three focused design screens that restructure payment comparison, coverage transparency, and item-level customisation.

Advance Payment Selection

User Problem Customers needed to evaluate their financial commitment before checkout, but could not compare monthly versus advance rental costs early in the journey.
Design Decision Introduce an advance payment selector directly on the PDP, allowing customers to compare monthly rental rates against advance savings for any selected tenure.

Rationale: Exposing payment options at the product level replaces unexpected post-checkout customer service calls with an early, optional user decision.

Protection Plan Visibility

User Problem Customers worried about accidental damage during their rental, but had no transparent breakdown of coverage boundaries or deduction rules.
Design Decision Surface the Protection Plan directly in the cart with unambiguous coverage parameters and the clear ability to opt out.
Covered Damage
  • Scratches and dents
  • Liquid spills
  • Cracks and tears
  • Bugs and fungus
Not Covered
  • Intentional damage
  • Cigarette burns
  • Harmful marks from sharp objects
  • Loss or theft of furniture

Rationale: Differentiating common accidental wear (spills, minor tears) from non-covered actions prevents end-of-subscription disputes and restores confidence.

Giving Users Control at the Cart Level

User Problem Customers needing to adjust tenure, quantity, or protection for individual items originally had to remove products or restart checkout.
Design Decision Provide an “Edit Item Preferences” bottom-sheet drawer that lets users adjust item settings without leaving the cart.

Rationale: Modular configuration inside the cart keeps users anchored to their purchase, ensuring line-items, rent, deposit, and totals update upon saving.

Trade-offs & Constraints

Balancing user autonomy against business risk and operational realities required navigating four distinct design tensions:

User choice versus backend credit checks

The desired experience was to let users understand and choose payment options in the digital flow. However, eligibility and risk-related decisions remained linked to backend credit checks, manual reviews, and varying CIBIL scores. Exposing choices earlier clarifies expectations but does not remove underlying verification rules.

Transparency versus operational dependency

Users needed clearer information and status visibility, while the original process relied heavily on Credit Team reviews and offline customer calls. The redesign aimed to reduce surprise decisions and make options clearer before checkout, though operational touchpoints and physical pickup inspections remained part of the business model.

Item-level flexibility versus checkout complexity

The per-item configuration model introduces explicit choices—tenure, protection, and payment plan—for each product. While this grants users granular control, it also increases interaction density, making a compact, understandable item-editing experience critical.

Clear protection information versus policy complexity

Coverage and exclusions needed to be understandable before users opted in. The interface had to present practical examples of covered and excluded damage clearly without oversimplifying them into a misleading promise of universal protection.

Reflection & Next Steps

What I learned

Holistic customer communication

Working on advance payments and protection plans highlighted the critical importance of communicating terms across the full customer lifecycle—from the PDP to final pickup.

Cross-functional stakeholder alignment

The design work helped bridge business, engineering, and operations by aligning stakeholders around tangible user needs rather than internal assumptions.

Balancing efficiency with user-centred clarity

Breaking complex workflows into clear user stories shifted our approach from pure operational gating to user-centred clarity, balancing trust and control against technical feasibility.

Next steps (Proposed future work)

As the feature was under development in the supplied context, proposed next steps for the product team include:

  • Usability testing: Conduct structured user sessions once the feature is live to observe real-world interactions.
  • Cohort adoption tracking: Measure longitudinal adoption rates for the Advance Plan and Protection Plan across user segments.
  • Funnel drop-off analysis: Monitor verification and checkout drop-offs to pinpoint residual friction.
  • Support query monitoring: Track customer service ticket volume around credit reviews and damage deductions.
  • Microcopy iteration: Refine copy around deposit settlements and edge-case damage scenarios based on post-launch feedback.

“Good subscription design doesn’t hide operational complexity; it resolves it into clear, confident choices before the customer ever commits.”
