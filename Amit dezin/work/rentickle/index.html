Case Study · Rental Commerce

Rentickle — Checkout Experience Redesign

Making rental costs clearer and checkout faster

Rentickle is a furniture and appliance rental platform that lets customers rent products through monthly payments instead of making large upfront purchases.

During my two-month project, I worked on simplifying the mobile checkout experience through competitive research, UX audits, behavioural data, customer interviews, and product data. The goal was to make the full rental commitment easier to understand and remove unnecessary friction from checkout.

Role Product Designer
Timeline 2 Months
Tools Figma, Clarity, GPT
Team Freelance project — Me (Product Designer), 1 Senior Designer, 1 Developer

Consolidated Rentickle mobile checkout: streamlined single-page interface with clear rental cost breakdown.

01 — Project Context

Why rental checkout needs more explanation than a typical purchase

Rentickle’s rental model requires customers to understand recurring monthly rent, advance payments, refundable security deposits, additional charges, and identity verification.

Unlike a simple one-time purchase, the customer needs to understand both the payment due today and the ongoing rental commitment. The checkout must make those details clear before asking the customer to commit.

The Rental Model & Operational Gating

In conventional e-commerce, the final number is the complete transaction. In rental commerce, checkout initiates a multi-month financial and legal commitment. If customers cannot instantly decouple what is charged today from what will be billed on the 1st of every month, apprehension stops the order.

02 — Problem Statement

Checkout was taking too long, and too few users were completing it

The data revealed severe attrition: average checkout duration stretched past six minutes, and fewer than one in ten users who entered checkout completed their booking.

Baseline checkout performance

Prior to the redesign, performance metrics highlighted major barriers to completion:

Checkout Duration 6m 22s Average session time before redesign
Completion Rate 9.8% Orders placed versus checkout entries
KYC Drop-off High Abandonment at identity-verification step

Three main friction areas

  • Pricing anxiety: Customers struggled to reconcile monthly rent with the full amount due at checkout.
  • Form fatigue: Repeated address entry and an unnecessary billing-address step increased input effort.
  • Visual and interaction friction: Redundant layouts, unclear protection-plan states, and limited feedback made checkout harder to navigate.
Design Challenge

“How might we make rental checkout easier to understand and complete without removing the information and verification customers need?”

We used behavioural data to find where checkout lost clarity

Friction Audit 03

KYC & Form Friction

“The KYC document-upload field had the highest rage-click concentration, while billing-address and protection-plan interactions revealed additional friction. These findings helped direct the redesign toward clearer guidance and fewer unnecessary steps.”

  • Rage clicks on KYC upload: The KYC page had the highest rage-click concentration on the document-upload field, suggesting confusion about accepted formats or file-size limits.
  • Widespread billing form abandonment: The billing-address form was visited and abandoned by a large segment of sessions, despite only approximately 2% of orders requiring a distinct billing address.
  • Dead clicks on protection plan: Dead clicks clustered around the protection plan, suggesting uncertainty about whether it was preselected, included, or optional.
04 — User Interviews

We spoke to customers to understand why they hesitated

“Behavioural data showed where users interacted with checkout, but it could not explain why they hesitated. We spoke to six recent customers and four customers who had abandoned checkout to better understand their experiences.”

Research Scope

10 participants in total: 6 recent customers who completed checkout + 4 customers who abandoned during the checkout journey.

Quote 01 — Pricing Uncertainty

“I didn’t know I had to pay a deposit on top of the monthly rent. I thought checkout would show me the total.”

Interpretation The customer expected the full financial commitment to be visible before reaching the payment decision.
Quote 02 — Repeated Address Entry

“It kept asking me for the same address again. I wasn’t sure if it saved my earlier input.”

Interpretation Repeated input and uncertainty about whether information had been retained created unnecessary effort.
Quote 03 — Protection-Plan Confusion

“I wasn’t sure what the protection plan covered. I selected it, deselected it, then left.”

Interpretation The selection state and value of the protection plan were not sufficiently clear to this participant.
05 — Research Synthesis

Three friction patterns shaped the redesign

Connecting behavioural telemetry to qualitative feedback clarified three systemic UX blockers. Each finding directly determined a specific architectural direction.

A

Opaque pricing structure

What we found Customers could not easily reconcile monthly rent with the amount due at checkout. The deposit, first month’s rent, and setup fee appeared at different stages instead of together.
Design direction Show Pay Today and Your Monthly Pay together from the beginning of checkout. Make refundable deposits, advance rent, charges, and discounts easy to understand.
B

Multi-page form fragmentation

What we found Address, billing address, KYC, and payment were spread across four separate pages without a persistent progress indicator or summary. Going back cleared subsequent inputs on some paths.
Design direction Consolidate the primary checkout into one scrollable interface where appropriate, use inline validation, and avoid destructive back-navigation.
C

Unnecessary cognitive load

What we found The billing-address form appeared for everyone even though approximately 2% of orders required a different billing address. The protection plan appeared mandatory or unclear, and the KYC upload lacked sufficient guidance.
Design direction Default to the shipping address and reveal separate billing options when required. Make the protection-plan selection and value clearer. Add inline KYC file-format and size guidance.
Note: The persistent protection-plan reminder did not ship in V1 and is documented under Trade-offs, not as an implemented feature.
06 — User Flow

Simplifying the main checkout path

“We mapped the user journey to identify repeated steps and secondary actions that interrupted the main path. The redesign simplified the default journey while preserving essential login, address-selection, and payment requirements.”

Observed Original Flow (6 steps)

Cart → Login → Checkout → Address → Agree to Terms → Proceed to Pay

Redesigned Main Path (3 consolidated steps)

Checkout → Confirm Address → Proceed to Payment

Complete Flow Logic & Conditional Branches

  • Logged-out users: Directed through login/signup authentication and seamlessly returned to their checkout state without losing form data.
  • New users: Prompted to add their delivery address and select it immediately.
  • Returning users: Saved address selected by default; secondary saved addresses accessible via a lightweight drawer.
  • Coupon application: Optional inline accordion — keeps discounts accessible without interrupting the primary payment path.
  • Secondary “Update Items” branch: Updating cart items, tenures, and quantities remains available as a secondary action without leaving the checkout page context.

User flow architecture: Original 6-step flow compared against the simplified 3-step primary path and conditional branches.

07 — Design Changes

Design Changes

Guided by behavioural drop-off data and customer interview feedback, three targeted design changes restructured the checkout experience to deliver immediate clarity and eliminate unnecessary input steps.

Making Rental Pricing Transparent

Problem Customers needed to understand the financial commitment but could not easily distinguish the amount due now from the recurring monthly payment.
Evidence Behavioural data highlighted activity around pricing and coupons, while interviews revealed uncertainty about refundable deposits.
Design Decision Create a unified pricing breakdown that distinguishes Pay Today from Your Monthly Pay and clearly identifies the refundable deposit and applicable charges.
The intended design includes:
  • Upfront payment and recurring monthly rent
  • Clearly labelled refundable deposit
  • Relevant fees, taxes, advance rent, and discounts
  • Protection-plan information where applicable

Transparent rental pricing: Two-horizon cost architecture distinguishing initial outlay from recurring rent.

Design Rationale Distinguishing the initial payment due today from ongoing monthly rent directly resolved the primary quote friction (“I didn’t know I had to pay a deposit on top of the monthly rent”). Customers evaluate rental commitments on affordability; providing unambiguous line-items for deposits, taxes, and advance rent restores certainty before payment commitment.

Consolidating Checkout into One Page

Problem The original checkout required users to navigate separate screens and repeat or revisit information.
Evidence The audit identified low visibility of detailed pricing, repeated input, and friction associated with separate address and billing steps.
Design Decision Consolidate the main checkout into a focused, scrollable interface. Simplify address presentation, group order details, keep coupon access available, and make the primary payment action easier to find.
Flow Comparison
Original flow: Cart → Login → Checkout → Address → Agree to Terms → Proceed to Pay
Redesigned flow: Checkout → Confirm Address → Proceed to Payment
Login/signup, address selection, and payment requirements remain available as needed.

Before & After: Transition from a fragmented multi-page flow to a single unified scrollable checkout view.

Design Rationale Multi-step page reloads destroyed contextual awareness: users lost visibility of their cart and pricing while entering delivery details, triggering hesitation and back-navigation that frequently wiped form state. Consolidating into a single page provides continuous visual reference, allowing users to verify line-items while confirming delivery.

Removing the Separate Billing-Address Step

Evidence Product data showed that approximately 2% of B2C orders used a billing address different from the shipping address.
Design Decision Default to the shipping address and move billing options inside the address-selection drawer. Preserve the ability to select or enter a separate billing address when necessary.

Address drawer: Default shipping selection with nested optional billing address input for edge cases.

Design Rationale Forcing 100% of customers through a dedicated billing-address screen to support a 2% operational exception was a major driver of form fatigue and abandonment. Defaulting to shipping eliminates a full interaction hurdle for 98% of users while fully preserving compliance and accounting support for corporate or alternative billing requests via progressive disclosure.
08 — Trade-offs

Three useful ideas could not ship in V1

Good product design requires disciplined scoping. We explicitly prioritized stability and core completion over secondary convenience features during the two-month implementation cycle.

Concept What we wanted Why it did not ship
Coupon states Automatically show eligible coupons as active and explain why ineligible coupons cannot be used. Backend conditional logic was not ready for V1.
Protection-plan visibility Keep the protection-plan state clear, especially for returning customers. Business priorities and timing pushed the persistent reminder out of scope.
Preselected address map Make address selection and delivery-zone information more immediate and visual. Technical complexity was too high within the available timeline.

10 — Learnings

What I learned from working with real behaviour data

Designing within live product telemetry and real constraints reshaped my approach to interaction architecture:

Working with real data

This was my first hands-on experience with heatmaps and scroll maps. They changed how I evaluated interfaces by showing where users interacted with the page and where attention declined.

Research that removes

Some of the most valuable findings led to removing fields, steps, and decisions rather than adding features. Simplification became a deliberate product decision.

Understanding limitations earlier

Features that did not ship reinforced the importance of understanding business priorities, technical complexity, and backend readiness during scoping.

“Good checkout design is not about asking customers to move faster through complexity. It is about removing the complexity they should never have had to navigate in the first place.”
