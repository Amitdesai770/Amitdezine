HDFC Bank · Payment Gateway

Designing a clearer,
more reassuring payment experience

A payment gateway does more than process a transaction. It shapes how confidently a shopper can review an order, choose a payment method, and understand what happens when they pay.

Role UX Design Intern
Timeline May – July 2024 · 12 weeks
Industry Fintech
Collaboration HDFC Bank design team & mentor
Scope Shopper-facing payment gateway

During my 12-week UX Design internship at HDFC Bank, I explored how an established banking brand could translate its reputation for reliability into a clear, intuitive shopper-facing payment experience. Through competitive analysis, user interviews, and iterative design, I developed an end-to-end desktop payment flow and selected mobile screens.

Deliverables: Competitive audit, user research, information architecture, wireframes, desktop prototype, selected mobile screens, and a design presentation to the VPs of Design.

01

Project Context

Small and medium-sized businesses (SMBs) often rely on third-party payment gateways instead of building and maintaining their own payment infrastructure. These solutions allow businesses to accept digital payments without taking on the full technical and operational cost of managing a payment system.

For shoppers, however, checkout introduces a critical moment in the purchase journey. They need to understand the transaction, recognise their payment options, and feel confident about the experience before committing their money.

HDFC Bank's established reputation for banking and financial services presented an opportunity to explore a payment gateway that combined brand recognition with a straightforward checkout experience. But brand recognition alone would not make the experience effective — the payment interface still needed to present information clearly, support different payment preferences, and communicate transaction outcomes without unnecessary friction.

The opportunity: translate HDFC Bank's existing brand confidence into a payment experience that shoppers could understand and navigate with ease.

02

Problem Statement

The initial business ambition was to explore how HDFC Bank could establish itself as a dependable payment gateway provider. I narrowed my design focus to the shopper-facing checkout experience.

How might HDFC Bank design a payment experience that helps shoppers understand their transactions, choose payment methods confidently, and complete payments through a clear, intuitive flow — while accommodating different merchant brands?

This framing brought three priorities into focus:

TrustHelp shoppers feel reassured during the transaction
UsabilityClear information hierarchy
IntuitivenessUnderstandable at every stage

These became the principles guiding my competitive analysis, user interviews, and subsequent design decisions.

03

Design Process

I worked on the payment gateway alongside another project during my internship. With limited time available, I followed a structured process to understand existing experiences, investigate user behaviour, identify design opportunities, and develop the proposed solution.

  • Competitive analysis of payment gateways and merchant checkout experiences
  • One-on-one interviews with six users
  • Synthesis of findings and identification of design priorities
  • Information architecture and low-fidelity wireframes
  • Design exploration, mentor feedback, and iteration
  • Final desktop payment flow, selected mobile screens, and prototype presentation

The objective was to use research to guide the design direction rather than move directly from competitor screenshots to visual design.

04

Competitive Analysis

I reviewed Stripe, Razorpay, Juspay, and Paytm, examining their payment experiences across merchants such as Nykaa, BookMyShow, Kylie Cosmetics, Swiggy, Starbucks, and Myntra.

I evaluated the experiences across four dimensions:

  • Feature breadth and data flows — How payment options were presented and how users progressed through the transaction
  • Interface layouts and user flows — How payment information was structured and how much was visible at once
  • Branding and merchant customisation — How the gateway's identity worked alongside the merchant's brand
  • Usability and accessibility — How clearly the interface communicated choices, errors, and next actions
05

Competitive Analysis Findings

The audit revealed different approaches to branding, layout, payment selection, and transaction feedback. Eight design implications emerged from comparing gateways and merchant implementations.

Merchant-specific customisation

Stripe, Juspay, and Razorpay offered options for adapting the interface to individual merchant brands.

Design Implication

Balance HDFC Bank's recognisable identity with a clean, merchant-friendly presentation that supports future customisation.

Layout optimisation

Stripe's text-heavy single-page presentation and Razorpay's three-column layout offered different approaches to organising checkout information.

Design Implication

Explore how information could be organised into clearer sections without introducing unnecessary steps.

White-label products

White-label solutions allow businesses to offer a payment experience under their own brand without building the entire system themselves.

Design Implication

Merchant adaptability is an important product consideration, alongside clear payment information and a recognisable gateway identity.

Order summary visibility

Comparison of the Starbucks and Nykaa experiences highlighted differences in how order details were presented before payment.

Design Implication

A clear order summary could help shoppers review the transaction and understand what they are paying for before proceeding.

Recommended payment methods

Juspay and Razorpay provided ways to bring certain payment methods into prominent view.

Design Implication

Recommendations could reduce search effort, but should not make other available methods difficult to find.

Error-state visibility

Opportunities to improve error visibility identified in the BookMyShow and Nykaa experiences, where merchant styling competed with error indicators.

Design Implication

Error messages need a clear visual hierarchy and timely feedback, helping users recognise a problem and understand how to correct it.

Compact gateway layouts

Some Juspay experiences used compact pop-up payment forms, giving the checkout a more focused presentation.

Design Implication

A streamlined interface could reduce visual clutter while preserving access to essential payment and order information.

Balanced payment-method visibility

Experiences differed in how prominently individual payment methods were presented, including proprietary options.

Design Implication

The interface needed to make payment methods recognisable without unnecessarily prioritising one option over others.

Key Takeaway

The design opportunity was not to reproduce a competitor's layout, but to combine a clear information hierarchy, recognisable payment choices, adaptable branding, and visible transaction feedback in a coherent checkout experience.

06

User Interviews

To complement the competitive audit, I conducted six one-on-one qualitative interviews alongside my design mentor at HDFC Bank. Within the internship timeline, I aimed to include variation in participants' age, gender, location, and occupation.

The interviews explored three areas:

  • User behaviour and practices — Shopping habits, device usage, and commonly used payment methods and gateways
  • Current experiences and pain points — Checkout difficulties, expectations around usability, support, and reliability
  • Security and trust — Concerns about online transactions and what influenced their trust

Six interviews provided qualitative insights into individual experiences and recurring themes. The findings were exploratory rather than statistically representative.

07

Interview Findings & Insights

The interviews surfaced concerns around familiar brands, refunds, payment preferences, stored card details, scams, digital confidence, and device usage. Eight themes shaped my understanding of payment behaviour.

Trust in known brands

Participants described greater confidence in established brands and hesitation toward unfamiliar sellers, particularly those discovered through social media advertisements.

I hesitate to buy products that come on Facebook ads

I trust brands like Amazon, Flipkart

UX Takeaway

Brand familiarity can influence how shoppers approach a transaction. For HDFC Bank, this raised the opportunity to use recognisable payment branding as a reassurance cue without overwhelming the merchant's identity.

Refunds influence confidence

Participants described how the refund experience shaped their perception of a platform, particularly when resolving an unsuccessful transaction required contacting support.

If refunds are fast it helps to keep trust

Its painful to keep contacting support team

UX Takeaway

Confidence extends beyond the payment action itself. Clear transaction feedback matters, while refund speed remains a broader service consideration a checkout interface alone cannot guarantee.

Payment preferences vary by transaction amount

UPI was described as convenient for everyday payments, while some participants preferred net banking or cards for larger amounts.

Who will carry their card everywhere?

I send amounts above 5-10k through netbanking

UX Takeaway

Payment choice is contextual. The interface should make available methods easy to recognise and navigate rather than assuming one method suits every situation.

Cash on delivery can outweigh promotional offers

Some participants valued the ability to inspect an item before paying and considered this reassurance more important than discounts or cashback.

I prefer checking the parcel before paying

So the offers dont matter much

UX Takeaway

Incentives alone may not address a user's underlying concerns. The design should present relevant information clearly rather than assume promotional visibility drives payment behaviour.

Users are cautious about saving card details

Participants raised concerns about who would store their information and whether they could control future deductions.

Who is saving this information?

There should be a verification before auto-deduct

I feel risky to save my card details

UX Takeaway

Convenience needs to be balanced with control. Clear explanations and appropriate consent around saved payment details are important design considerations.

Scams contribute to payment anxiety

Participants described concerns about phishing and OTP scams, particularly among people with less familiarity or confidence using digital banking.

My wife got in a OTP scam and lost money

This OTP thing is very scary

I hear older people say there is a lot of scams

UX Takeaway

Payment instructions and security-related messages need to be understandable. A checkout experience should avoid adding ambiguity to an already sensitive interaction.

Older users can encounter usability barriers

Participants described difficulties with small touch targets, multiple windows, account management, and remembering steps in the payment process.

I misclick a lot

I cant handle multiple windows

I forgot to add a beneficiary in the process

UX Takeaway

A familiar payment task can still become difficult when navigation is unclear or interactions require users to remember too much. The design needed to consider clarity and understandable next steps.

Smartphones shape payment behaviour

Participants described using smartphones for everyday payments and sometimes switching from desktop shopping to mobile payment methods.

Who will carry a laptop everywhere?

I scan QR using my phone from the laptop screen

UX Takeaway

Device context matters. The desktop checkout needed to remain understandable while recognising that some users might rely on their phones to complete payment-related actions.

Research Synthesis

Across these findings, a recurring theme was the importance of confidence and control. Participants' concerns were not limited to whether a platform looked trustworthy; they also related to understanding the transaction, choosing a suitable payment method, protecting financial information, and knowing what happened after paying.

08

Design Priorities

The competitor audit highlighted opportunities in layout, payment-method visibility, order summaries, and branding. The interviews added context about how people assessed payment options and what made them feel uncertain.

I used these findings to focus the design on two connected areas: trust and reassurance, and usability and flow optimisation.

Trust & Reassurance
  • Recognisable payment cues — familiar payment-method branding to help shoppers identify options
  • HDFC Bank brand confidence — bank's established identity could reassure shoppers on less familiar merchant platforms
  • Neutral, merchant-friendly presentation — HDFC branding visible but restrained
Usability & Flow Optimisation
  • Clear information flow — understandable sections and stages, avoiding unnecessary complexity
  • Transparent order details — order summary easy to find before paying
  • Focused interface — reduce visual clutter, prioritise information at each stage
09

Information Architecture

With the main design priorities established, I mapped the information architecture to determine how payment selection, navigation, order details, and the overall page layout should fit together.

This helped me consider the checkout as a connected journey rather than a collection of independent screens. The structure needed to make essential information accessible while helping shoppers understand what to do at each stage.

10

Wireframes & Iterations

I began with low-fidelity wireframes to explore different arrangements of payment methods, navigation, order summaries, and page layouts before committing to the final visual design.

This phase gave me room to test different structures and consider alternatives beyond the patterns identified in the competitive audit. Feedback from my design mentors encouraged me to remain open to different perspectives and continue exploring instead of settling too quickly on an initial concept.

The main considerations were how to balance information density with clarity, keep payment choices accessible, and maintain a focused layout without hiding essential transaction details.

11

Final Design & Prototype

The final design established an end-to-end desktop payment flow, from payment selection through transaction completion. I also created selected mobile screens to explore how the experience could translate to smaller devices.

The solution brought the research-led priorities into the interface through four key elements.

Order summary & transaction clarity

The order summary was designed to help shoppers review the transaction before proceeding — addressing the opportunity to make purchase details easier to find at the point of payment.

Payment-method selection

Recognisable payment-method branding helped establish clear visual distinctions between available options. The aim was to support quick scanning while keeping payment choices easy to discover.

HDFC Bank branding & merchant adaptability

The design explored a clean, merchant-friendly interface with a restrained HDFC Bank identity — balancing bank recognition with the need to preserve the merchant's presence in checkout.

Transaction completion feedback

Success feedback provided a clear endpoint to the proposed flow, helping communicate that the transaction had reached its completed state.

12

Mobile Screens

Alongside the desktop flow, I designed selected mobile screens to explore how key elements could adapt to smaller displays.

The mobile explorations retained the core considerations of payment-method recognition, order-summary visibility, offer placement, and transaction success feedback. The deliverable covered selected screens rather than a complete mobile flow.

13

Split Bills — Additional Feature Exploration

Alongside the main checkout project, I explored a Split Bills concept that emerged during project discussions and mentor feedback. The idea was to help users divide expenses among selected contacts and send reminders, reducing the effort involved in tracking contributions and following up manually.

The proposed feature focused on three needs:

  • Expense tracking — Keep track of individual contributions and split details
  • Easy division — Divide an expense and send reminders to selected contacts
  • Timely contributions — Make it easier to follow up when payments are due

Potential future directions included requesting contributions before payment via UPI, group splitting for expenses shared by multiple people, and automatic splitting for recurring payments.

I treated this as an additional product exploration rather than part of the core shopper-facing checkout flow. Further research would be needed to validate the specific use cases.

14

Future Scope & Success Metrics

The internship focused on producing a research-informed design flow and prototype within the available time. The proposed solution was not accompanied by measured production results, so its effect on conversion, transaction speed, or user trust remains unvalidated.

If the concept progressed to usability testing, I would evaluate it using four measures:

Checkout completion rate

Whether shoppers successfully complete the payment task and where they abandon the flow.

Time to complete a transaction

How efficiently users move through checkout while still understanding the transaction.

Error frequency and recovery

Where mistakes occur, how clearly errors are communicated, and whether users can recover without unnecessary effort.

User confidence and satisfaction

Whether shoppers understand the transaction outcome and feel confident about the experience.

15

Learnings

01
Balancing quality and timelines

Working across two projects strengthened my ability to focus on the most relevant problems and explore design alternatives within a constrained timeline.

02
Ownership and initiative

I took part in the project from competitive investigation and user interviews through information architecture, wireframing, and prototyping — connecting the research to the design direction.

03
Collaboration and communication

Working alongside my design mentor helped me consider alternative approaches, respond to feedback, and communicate design decisions more clearly. Presenting the work to HDFC Bank's VPs of Design gave me experience explaining the project and its rationale to senior stakeholders.

04
Iteration and learning

The wireframing phase reinforced the value of exploring alternatives before committing to a visual direction. Rather than treating the first concept as the final answer, I learned to remain open to feedback and reconsider how information and interactions could be organised.

A payment experience needs to do more than present payment options — it needs to help people understand the transaction, feel in control of their choices, and recognise what happens next.
